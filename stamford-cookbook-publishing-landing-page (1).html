Roast Chicken with Lemon, Garlic and Thyme

The first thing I learned to cook, and still the thing I make when I want the house to smell like a Sunday. Take the bird out of the refrigerator a full hour before it goes in the oven. That hour is most of the recipe.

Serves
4
Active
20 min
Total
1 hr 45 min
Oven
425°F

Ingredients

  • 1 whole chicken, about 4 lb
  • 2 lemons, one halved, one sliced
  • 1 head garlic, halved crosswise
  • 6 sprigs thyme
  • 3 tbsp butter, softened
  • 1½ tsp kosher salt
  • Black pepper, freshly ground

Method

  1. 1Heat the oven to 425°F. Pat the chicken dry inside and out and season it all over with the salt and a generous grinding of pepper.
  2. 2Push the halved lemon, the garlic and four sprigs of thyme into the cavity. Rub the butter over the skin and tuck the lemon slices underneath.
  3. 3Roast for 1 hour 15 minutes, basting once at the halfway mark, until the juices at the thigh run clear.

Why cookbooks are different

A cookbook is the hardest kind of book to publish well.

A novel has one voice and one column of text. A cookbook has hundreds of numbers, two hundred ingredient names, a photography budget, a color press, and a reader holding it open with a wet hand. Most of what goes wrong with a cookbook happens long before the printer sees the file.

What usually goes wrong

The eight faults we find most often in a first draft

  • Measurements drift between recipes. A cup here, 240 ml there, a knob of butter in chapter four.
  • Ingredients listed out of the order they are used, so the cook keeps scanning back up the page.
  • Photos shot across different months and different light, so the food changes color through the book.
  • Interiors printed in one ink to save money, which turns every dish gray.
  • A perfect bound spine that snaps shut the second you let go of it.
  • No ingredient index, so nobody can find the recipe that uses up the leftover buttermilk.
  • Yields, times and oven temperatures that were never checked by a second pair of hands.
  • Files sent to press without bleed, so photos are trimmed into thin white edges.

What we do about it

The matching pass in every Stamford project

  • Every measurement standardized to one system, with the second system in parentheses if you want both.
  • Ingredient lists reordered to match the order of use, and grouped when a recipe has components.
  • A written photography brief that fixes one light direction, one surface palette and one prop set.
  • Four color interiors with a proofed color target, so the food on page 90 matches the food on page 12.
  • Binding chosen for how the book is actually used, and priced against your run length.
  • A two level index, by recipe name and by main ingredient, built after the pages are final.
  • A recipe check pass for yield, timing, temperature and step continuity before anything is designed.
  • Print ready PDF/X files with bleed, trim marks and the printer's own specification applied.

Included in a cookbook project

Everything between your recipes and a finished book.

You can take the whole path or any part of it. Most authors arrive with recipes and a strong point of view, and need the other eight things on this list.

Recipe editing and standardization

Every recipe read for consistent format, measurement, yield, temperature and step order. Ingredient names normalized so scallions are scallions from the first page to the last.

Copyediting and proofreading

Two editorial passes on headnotes, front matter and back matter, then a proofread on the laid out pages, which is where cookbook errors actually hide.

Food photography direction

A shot list, prop and surface palette, light direction and a style guide your photographer can shoot from. If you already have images, we sort, retouch and color correct what is usable.

Interior layout and typesetting

Recipe templates that hold up across one page, two page and full spread recipes, with room for notes, variations and a cook who leans a spoon on the corner.

Cover and jacket design

Front, spine and back designed together, with the spine width calculated from your final page count and paper stock rather than guessed at.

Print production

Trim size, paper weight, coating, binding and color proofing, then press ready files. We speak to the printer so nothing arrives cropped, dark or off color.

Ebook and fixed layout

A reflowable ebook where the recipes lead, and a fixed layout edition when the design has to survive the transfer. Both tested on real devices.

ISBN, copyright and metadata

ISBN assignment, copyright registration support, BISAC categories, keywords and the description fields that decide where your book turns up in a search.

Distribution and launch

Retail listings, print on demand fulfillment, bookstore ordering channels, review copies and a launch plan sized to what you actually want the book to do.

The physical book

Choose the book before you design the book.

Binding, trim size and paper decide the price, the shelf presence and whether the book will sit open next to a mixing bowl. These choices come first, because the layout has to be built for them.

Lay flat softcover

A sewn or Otabind construction where the cover is glued only at the edges, so the block opens flat and stays there without being held.

Best for working cookbooks that live on a counter and get used weekly.

Concealed wire-o

Wire binding hidden behind a printed spine, so the book looks like a hardcover on a shelf and opens like a spiral on a bench.

Best for heavily photographed books that need to fold right back.

Case bound hardcover

Board covers with sewn sections, a printed jacket or a foiled case. The heaviest, longest lasting and most giftable of the four.

Best for chef books, heirloom collections and anything sold as a present.

Perfect bound softcover

The standard glued paperback. The cheapest per copy and the fastest to reprint, but the spine will close on you unless you crack it.

Best for text led books, fundraisers and large first runs on a tight budget.

Who we publish

Eight kinds of cookbook, each with its own set of problems.

  • Family and heirloom collections

    Handwritten cards, index boxes and half remembered quantities, turned into a book the whole family can cook from. Transcription and recipe reconstruction included.

  • Chef and restaurant books

    Service recipes scaled down to a home kitchen without losing the dish. Yield conversion, equipment substitution and technique notes for the reader who does not own a blast chiller.

  • Health, diet and clinical nutrition

    Nutritional analysis per serving, allergen labeling, macro breakdowns and careful handling of any health claim that appears anywhere in the text.

  • Baking and pastry

    The least forgiving category. Weight first measurement, baker's percentages, dough temperature notes and the step photography that baking books live or die on.

  • Regional and cultural cooking

    Non English ingredient names set with consistent transliteration and diacritics, a sourcing appendix, and a glossary that respects the original language.

  • Community and fundraising books

    Many contributors, many formats, one voice. Contributor permissions, credit lines and a print run priced so the fundraiser actually raises funds.

  • Single subject and technique books

    One ingredient, one method or one piece of equipment, carried across a whole book. Structure matters more here than anywhere else.

  • Brand and product cookbooks

    Books built around a product line or a farm, with brand guidelines applied to the design and a distribution plan that includes your own channels.

Before you call

The questions cookbook authors ask first.

How much does it cost to publish a cookbook?

Three things move the number more than anything else: how many recipes there are, whether the interior prints in full color, and whether you need photography commissioned rather than supplied. A sixty recipe full color book with a commissioned shoot sits at a very different level from a hundred and twenty recipe text led book using your own images.

We do not publish a single package price for cookbooks, because the honest answer changes with the recipe count. After the consultation you get an itemized quote with every line broken out, so you can remove anything you would rather handle yourself.

Can I copyright my recipes?

A bare list of ingredients is generally not protected by copyright on its own. What is protected is your expression: the headnotes, the instructions written in your voice, the selection and arrangement of the collection, and the photographs and design. That is one of the reasons headnotes matter so much in a cookbook, and one of the reasons we spend editorial time on them.

General information about how cookbook copyright usually works, not legal advice. For your specific situation, speak to an intellectual property attorney.

Do I need professional food photography?

No, but color photography changes how a cookbook sells, and readers do decide with their eyes. There are four routes and all of them work: a full commissioned shoot, a partial shoot covering only the hero recipes, illustration instead of photography, or a curated and retouched selection of images you already own.

If you shoot it yourself, we write the brief first so the images are consistent enough to sit together in one book. Inconsistent photography is harder to fix later than almost anything else.

How many recipes should my cookbook have?

Most trade cookbooks land between sixty and a hundred and twenty. Below sixty the book can feel slight at a normal price point, and above a hundred and twenty it starts to lose its shape unless there is a strong organizing idea holding it together.

Recipe count drives page count, page count drives spine width and unit cost, and unit cost drives your retail price. This is worth settling early. It is almost always better to cut ten weak recipes than to pad with them.

Do my recipes need to be tested?

Every recipe in a published cookbook should have been cooked successfully by someone who did not write it. That is the standard, and it is what protects you from a one star review that begins with the words "the timing is wrong."

We work at three levels: a desk check for internal consistency, a structured test by a second cook, or full independent testing across a panel. Most first time authors choose the desk check plus testing on the recipes they are least sure about.

How long does a cookbook take?

Longer than a novel of the same length, because photography and color proofing are sequential and cannot be rushed without showing. A straightforward text led cookbook moves faster than a photo led one, and a book that needs a commissioned shoot is paced by the shoot.

We give you a dated schedule with the plan at stage two, and the two things most likely to move it are photography and how quickly approvals come back from you.

Can you work from handwritten recipe cards?

Yes, and it is one of the most common places a family cookbook starts. We transcribe the cards, standardize the measurements, fill the gaps that every handwritten recipe has, and send you a query list for the things only you can answer, like what "bake until done" meant in your grandmother's oven.

Where a card is illegible or a step is missing, we flag it rather than invent it. Nothing goes into your book that you have not confirmed.

Where will my cookbook be sold?

Major online retailers as print and ebook, wholesale catalogs that bookstores and libraries order from, and your own direct channels, which is where a cookbook author usually keeps the most margin per copy.

Print on demand covers the long tail with no inventory. If you want stock for events, markets, a restaurant counter or a farm shop, we can quote a short offset run alongside it.

What if I only need part of this?

Then you only pay for part of it. Plenty of authors come to us with a finished, designed book that needs print production sorted out, or with strong photography and no idea how to lay out a recipe page. Every stage on this page can be bought on its own.
